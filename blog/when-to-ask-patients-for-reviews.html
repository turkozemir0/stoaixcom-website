Reviews and reputation

When and how to ask patients for Google reviews

Most happy patients never write a review; unhappy ones do. That imbalance only changes when asking for reviews becomes a system rather than luck.

Published: 20 November 20263 min read

Short answer: Ask at the peak moment, when the patient expresses satisfaction in their own words, never during a difficult stage of recovery. Make it easy (a one-tap link), leave them free (no suggested wording, no is fine) and ask about their experience first, so unhappy patients reach your team before a review page. Incentivised reviews and scripted wording break Google's rules and UK consumer law.

Why happy patients don't write reviews

For a happy patient, treatment is over and life moves on. An unhappy one is still emotionally in the experience and wants to express it. Left alone, your reviews will not reflect real satisfaction. The fix is to ask happy patients at the right moment, in an easy way.

The right moment

TreatmentRight momentWrong moment
Whitening, hygieneSame or next dayDuring sensitivity
Implants, crownsAfter the final restoration is fittedThe first days after surgery
Hair transplantAfter the first check-up, or when results start to showDuring shock loss
Cosmetic surgeryWhen swelling has settled and the patient sees the resultThe first week
General consultationA few hours after the visitWeeks later

The common rule: the best moment is when the patient says, in their own words, that they are happy. "That was so much easier than I expected, thank you" is the moment to send the link.

Ask about the experience first

Before the review request, ask one simple question: "How would you rate your experience from 1 to 5?" or "Did everything go as you expected?" Send happy patients the review link; listen to unhappy ones first and pass them to your team.

This is not about stopping unhappy patients from reviewing; they are always free to. It is about hearing a complaint first and having the chance to fix it.

A good review request

"Emma, we're so glad your treatment went smoothly. If you'd like to share your experience with other patients, you can leave a few words here: [link]. Entirely up to you, of course."

  • One-tap link. Don't make the patient search for you on Google.
  • No suggested wording. Scripted reviews break platform rules and read as fake.
  • No pressure. "Entirely up to you" leaves a real choice.
  • No incentives. Discounts or gifts for reviews break Google's policies, and the UK's Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 treats incentivised reviews that are not disclosed as banned practices.

Reminders

If the patient hasn't clicked, one short reminder a few days later is fine. A second usually irritates.

Replying to a negative review

Keep it short, include no health information and move the conversation to a private channel: "We're sorry your experience wasn't what you expected. We'd like to talk to you directly; please contact us on this number." Your real audience is not the reviewer but the hundreds of future patients who will read the reply.

How STOAIX handles it

STOAIX sends a satisfaction question at a time set per treatment type. Happy patients receive a one-tap Google review link; unhappy patients are flagged to your team immediately and the conversation continues privately. No scripted wording, no incentives. See patient reviews.

Frequently asked questions

Is a small discount for a review a problem?

Yes. Google prohibits incentivised reviews and may remove them, and UK consumer law treats undisclosed incentivised reviews as unfair.

Is it ethical not to send unhappy patients the review link?

As long as you never stop them reviewing, yes. Hearing their problem first is better for both patient and clinic.

How many reviews are enough?

There is no fixed number. Recency and content matter more to patients than the total; a steady flow of new reviews is the strongest signal.
