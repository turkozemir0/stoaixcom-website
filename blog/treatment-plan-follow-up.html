Treatment plan follow-up: what to do after the plan is sent

Once the treatment plan has gone out, the work can feel done. In reality the decision is usually made right then, at a kitchen table the clinic never sees.

Published: 20 November 20267 min read

This article is part of the Patient enquiry management: from first message to booked consultation series.

Short answer: Following up a treatment plan is not about asking "have you decided?". It is about sending information that helps the patient decide. On day one, confirm the plan arrived and ask if there are questions; over the next two weeks, get in touch on set days, each time with something new; if the patient clearly postpones or declines, record it. The plan itself is part of the follow-up: it should open with the patient's goal and end with one next step.

What is treatment plan follow-up?

Treatment plan follow-up is the set of planned contacts a clinic makes with a patient after sending a treatment plan or quote, until they make a decision. It is the least visible and most decisive part of the enquiry process. By now the patient knows the clinic, has heard the clinician's view and has seen a figure; they are weighing it against family, budget and perhaps other clinics' quotes. The clinic is not at that table; only its plan and later messages are. Without follow-up, patients rarely decide against you; they postpone, and a postponed decision fades. Poor follow-up, the same "just checking in" repeated, irritates the patient and pushes them into silence. Good follow-up is a series of short contacts that anticipate the questions in the patient's mind, offer information they can share with family, and leave the decision with them.

First, the plan: a document that explains itself

Half of follow-up depends on how the plan is written. Patients usually read it when you are not there, so it must explain itself. The sections of a good treatment plan:

SectionContentWhy
1. Your goalThe patient's goal and concern in their own wordsShows the plan is personal
2. Clinician's assessmentA short, clear summaryExplains the clinical basis
3. OptionsTwo or three options where relevant, with differencesMoves the question from "yes/no" to "which"
4. ScopeWhat is and is not includedPrevents surprises later
5. Process and timingNumber of visits, duration, recoveryAnswers the practical planning question
6. Fees and paymentTotal, payment optionsComes after scope
7. Next stepOne clear step and a named contactMakes deciding easier

Pairing the plan with a short call or voice note makes it more likely the patient opens and reads it, and catches their first questions straight away. Clear written information on options, risks and fees also supports the informed consent conversation the clinician will have.

A 14-day follow-up calendar

This calendar is a starting template. The days may differ by treatment type; what matters is that the date and content of each contact are planned in advance.

DayChannelPurposeExample content
0Call or voice note plus written planDeliver the plan"I've sent your plan; let me run through the three most important points."
1MessageConfirm it arrived, catch questions"Were you able to open the plan? What's the first question that came to mind?"
3MessageAnswer a common question"The question we're asked most about this treatment is recovery; briefly, it goes like this…"
5Message or emailA summary for family"If you'd like to share it with your partner, here is a one-page summary of the plan."
7CallTalk through concerns"If something is holding you back, I'd be glad to talk it through."
10MessagePractical informationAppointment availability, parking, what the first visit is like
14MessageA gentle close or postponement"If now isn't the right time, that's completely fine; when would you like me to get back in touch?"

If the patient has not decided after day 14 and gave a date, open a task for that date. If not, move to a monthly nurture rhythm; see unresponsive patient enquiries.

If the patient replies at any point, the calendar stops and the coordinator continues the conversation. An automated message going out straight after the patient asked a question damages trust.

Three rules for writing follow-up messages

  1. Every message gives something. Information, convenience or an answer. "Just checking in" gives nothing.
  2. Every message ends with one question. An easy one that takes ten seconds to answer.
  3. Every message remembers the goal. "So it's done before the wedding" or "so you can eat comfortably again": a phrase that goes back to the patient's own words.

What to avoid in plan follow-up

  • Fake deadlines. "This price ends on Friday" is pressure if there is no genuine reason. It damages trust in a medical decision, and the CAP Code treats false urgency in marketing as misleading.
  • Unprompted discounts. Dropping the price without being asked suggests the first figure was not real.
  • Sending the same message again. Copy-and-paste follow-up makes the patient feel like an entry on a list.
  • Relying on one person's memory. If the calendar is not in the CRM, follow-up is the first thing forgotten in a busy week.
  • Pushing the decision on the clinician's behalf. "The dentist says you need this done straight away" should never be used unless the clinician has genuinely flagged clinical urgency.

Guarding against low or incomplete plans

Some losses at this stage come not from follow-up but from the plan itself. If possible additional procedures are not written in and appear at consultation, the patient feels misled. To prevent that, write items the clinician says "may be needed", such as bone grafting or an extra visit, as a separate scenario: "If bone grafting proves necessary at assessment, the plan changes as follows." An honest scenario is more trustworthy than a single low figure.

Measurement: from plan to decision

Two measures show whether follow-up is working:

  • Plan-to-decision rate: plans resulting in a booking or deposit / plans sent.
  • Time to decision: the median number of days between sending the plan and the decision.

If the rate is falling against your own history, look first at the plan's structure and then at the calendar. If time to decision is growing, ask whether your follow-up contacts are valuable enough to the patient. The wider picture is in patient enquiry conversion metrics.

Leave the calendar to software and the conversation to people

Running a 14-day calendar by hand for dozens of patients strains even the most disciplined coordinator. STOAIX lead follow-up runs the calendar on its own, tailors each message to the patient's goal and stage, and stops the automated sequence the moment the patient replies, handing the conversation to the coordinator. The content of the plan, fees and clinical decisions always stay with the team and the clinician.

Frequently asked questions

How many times should we follow up after sending a plan?

There is no single right number. A few contacts on set days over the first two weeks, each carrying something new, is a sensible start. What matters more than the number is that each contact is valuable to the patient. If they say they do not want further contact, stop immediately.

When should we call after a patient says "I'll think about it"?

There is usually an unanswered question behind "I'll think about it". A good next step is to ask gently what they will be thinking about and agree a date for the next contact together. More in handling patient objections.

Does offering a discount make follow-up easier?

It may move some patients in the short term, but it weakens the credibility of your fees and risks conflicting with advertising standards. Explaining the scope and payment options clearly is the healthier route.

Should the treatment plan go by WhatsApp or email?

Send it on the channel the patient is using; an email copy can be added for their records. Because the plan contains health information, use the clinic's business accounts and secure systems, not personal phones.
