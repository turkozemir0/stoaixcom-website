Speed to lead for clinics: why the first five minutes decide

An enquiry loses value by the hour. Why reply speed is conversion rather than courtesy, and how to make it part of the system instead of one person's good intentions.

Published: 20 November 20265 min read

Short answer: Aim for five minutes during opening hours and fifteen out of hours. That is an internal target, not a research figure; it keeps you safely inside the one-hour window where enquiries are many times more likely to turn into a real conversation. The clinic that replies first sets the terms every other clinic is compared against.

Why do patient enquiries go cold so quickly?

Most enquiry forms are filled in on a phone in the evening. At that moment the question is at its warmest, two or three other clinics are open in other tabs, and the patient has not spoken to anyone yet. That window does not stay open. Reply the next morning and it has either closed or someone else has already walked through it.

Clinics rarely lose these patients because they said the wrong thing. They lose them because they said the right thing too late. Many patients who "went elsewhere on price" never saw your price at all.

What does the research say?

The Harvard Business Review study "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads" (2011) used two data sets. In an audit of 2,241 US companies, only 37% replied to a web enquiry within an hour, 23% never replied, and those that did took 42 hours on average. In an analysis of 1.25 million leads, firms that tried to make contact within an hour were nearly seven times more likely to have a meaningful conversation with the decision maker than firms that tried an hour later, and more than sixty times more likely than firms that waited 24 hours or longer.

Read the numbers carefully: they describe the odds of starting a real conversation, not of closing a sale. The direction is still unambiguous. In healthcare it is sharper, because the same patient usually contacts several clinics on the same evening.

Why speed matters twice as much for clinics

  • Whoever speaks first frames the decision. The first person to talk to the patient explains what matters: the clinician's experience, recovery, number of visits. Later replies are judged against that frame.
  • Speed is the first proof of care. Patients read your reply time as a preview of how you will treat them after the procedure.
  • Every slow reply wastes ad spend. A lead that goes cold takes the cost of the click with it.

What to do in the first five minutes

  1. Read the enquiry (30 seconds). Name, treatment, anything they ticked on the form, the ad they came from, their local time.
  2. Send a personal first message. Use their name, say who you are, refer to one detail from the form and ask one easy question.
  3. Call if they asked for a call. If there is no answer, leave a short note: "I just tried you, feel free to reply here whenever suits."
  4. Log the time. You cannot improve a reply time you do not measure.

A first message never contains a price list, a PDF or a request for photos. Those end conversations rather than start them. We break down the structure of a good opener in a separate guide.

If you use an auto-reply, avoid "Your request has been received". Patients file that under "no reply". A message with a real name, a clear time for the follow-up and one question leaves a far better first impression.

Do not stop at one attempt

Speed is not only the first minute. If there is no reply, plan the next 48 hours: a second message a few hours later that adds something useful, a call attempt the next day at a time that suits the patient, and a low-pressure note after two days. Every touch should carry something new. "Did you see my message?" never goes out on its own. Longer follow-up rhythms are covered in our guide to following up after a quote.

Make speed a system, not a person

Most clinics that believe they are fast discover an average measured in hours once they check. Speed that relies on one person breaks on their day off. To make it stick you need a written target for each stage (first reply, after photos, after a quote), a visible owner for every enquiry with a named backup, and a weekly check of the last 20 enquiries: time received against time of the first human reply.

Frequently asked questions

How quickly should a clinic respond to a new enquiry?

Five minutes in opening hours and fifteen outside them is a good internal target. The research threshold is the first hour; after 24 hours the chance of starting a real conversation drops sharply.

Is an automatic reply enough for night-time enquiries?

As a bridge, yes; as a destination, no. It should carry a real name, a clear time for the follow-up and one question. To actually move the conversation forward you need evening cover or an assistant that can answer questions.

Should I give the price in the first message?

If the patient asks, give an honest range and explain what it depends on. If they have not asked, sending a price list first tends to end the conversation. See our guide to the price question.

How do I measure our reply time?

List the last 20 enquiries with the time each arrived and the time of the first human reply. Work out the average and the worst case, and repeat weekly.
