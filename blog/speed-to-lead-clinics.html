Speed to lead for clinics: why the first hour matters

Someone who messages your clinic is often messaging two or three others at the same time. Who replies first, and how, sets the tone for everything that follows.

Published: 20 November 20269 min read

This article is part of the Patient enquiry management: from first message to booked consultation series.

Short answer: Reply to a patient enquiry within minutes during opening hours and first thing the next morning out of hours. The most widely cited study on the subject found that businesses replying within the first hour were far more likely to reach a meaningful conversation than those replying later. The clinic's job is to make that speed a written standard that is measured, not a matter of luck.

What is speed to lead, and why measure it separately?

Speed to lead is the time between an enquiry reaching your clinic, by message, form or missed call, and the patient receiving the first meaningful reply. "Meaningful" matters here: an automatic "thanks, we've received your message" does not count, because it does not answer the question. The first reply that uses the patient's name, addresses what they asked and suggests a next step does. It deserves its own measurement because it is the first moment at which everything else you invest in, from advertising and your website to your clinicians' reputation, either pays off or does not. At that moment the person is at their most curious and most ready, and often has not yet spoken to another clinic. A few hours later, the same person has either heard back from someone else or gone back to their day. Response time is therefore the simplest indicator of how your front of house actually works: if the median is long, the cause is usually missing ownership, missing capacity or no plan for out-of-hours enquiries.

What the research actually says

The most cited source on response speed is "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads", published in Harvard Business Review in 2011. It is worth saying up front that it contains no clinic data; it looks at how US companies responded to web enquiries. It is still a useful starting point for understanding the mechanism:

  • Of the 2,241 companies examined, only 37% responded to an enquiry within an hour and 23% never responded.
  • The same analysis reported an average first response time of 42 hours.
  • Companies that tried to contact the enquirer within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to have a meaningful conversation with a decision-maker as those that waited longer, and more than sixty times as likely as those that waited a day or more (HBR).

Translating this into "reply in five minutes and your conversion goes up by X" would be wrong. The study is from a different sector and a different decade, and it shows association, not cause: companies that replied quickly may simply have been better run in other ways too. The lesson that does carry over is this: interest should be met while it is warm, and organisations that do not measure their response time are usually much slower than they think.

Five reasons clinics reply slowly

  1. An inbox nobody owns. When everyone checks Instagram and nobody checks WhatsApp, messages are answered as they are noticed, not in order.
  2. Enquiries arriving in different places. If lead forms go to one email address, the website form to another and WhatsApp to a single phone, response time drops to the speed of the slowest channel.
  3. The phone comes first. Reception rightly looks after the ringing phone and the patient at the desk first, which means written enquiries wait for hours.
  4. The out-of-hours gap. Many people enquire in the evening and at weekends. Without a plan, the first reply lands the next afternoon.
  5. "Let me ask the dentist." Even simple questions are passed to the clinician and parked. The first reply does not need to wait for a clinical view; getting to know the patient and explaining the process is the coordinator's job.

Rather than looking for an industry benchmark, set a written standard that fits your capacity. The table is a starting suggestion; fill in your own values:

You do not have to write five minutes or fifteen minutes. What matters is that the number is realistic, written down and measured. If your capacity cannot meet it, add help for out-of-hours and peak times rather than loosening the standard.

How to measure response time

Measurement is simple but needs discipline. Keep three timestamps on every enquiry:

  • T0: when the message, call or form reached the clinic.
  • T1: when the first meaningful reply (from a person or an assistant) was sent.
  • T2: when the first human contact happened (tracked separately if an assistant replied first).

Use the median, not the mean. A few overnight messages with long waits inflate the mean and hide daytime performance; the median shows what a typical patient goes through. That is also why out-of-hours deserves its own line.

A first-60-minutes checklist

When a new enquiry arrives, the first hour should cover:

  1. Is there a record? Name, channel, source and first message are in the CRM.
  2. Is there an owner? One person's name is on it.
  3. Has the first reply gone? Using the patient's name, saying who is writing, with a short answer to the question.
  4. Has one question been asked? Something easy, such as "When are you hoping to have this done?" or "Have you had a consultation about this before?".
  5. Is it the right channel? If they wrote on WhatsApp, reply there; if they filled in a form, call first and text if there is no answer.
  6. Did a missed call leave a trace? If the phone wasn't picked up: "I've just tried to call you; feel free to reply here whenever suits you."
  7. Is the next task dated? If there is no reply, when and on which channel you will try again is written down.
  8. If a clinician is needed, has a time been given? "I've passed your photos to Dr Patel and will come back to you by 2pm tomorrow."

Each item takes under two minutes. What takes time is not doing the list but noticing that it was not done, so the list should be visible on the enquiry record in your CRM.

Speed and quality belong together

A fast but empty reply is no better than a slow, full one. Replying instantly with "Hi, how can I help?" simply asks the patient to repeat the question they already wrote. The value of speed is that the patient feels attended to, and that only happens when the question is answered. For the structure of a good first reply, see first reply templates for patient enquiries.

Speed should also not be confused with pressure. A fast clinic does not mean the patient must decide fast. Replying instantly and then saying "this price is only valid today" damages trust and sits badly with the CAP Code, which expects marketing not to mislead or exploit urgency, and with what professional regulators expect of healthcare providers.

Fast but human: where automation fits

The realistic way to meet a minutes-level standard in the evenings and at weekends is to let an AI assistant give the first reply. The assistant answers using information the clinic has approved, gathers the basics and opens the record; in the morning the coordinator picks up where it left off. STOAIX lead follow-up gives that first reply within seconds and logs every conversation in the CRM with a named owner.

The assistant's limits should also be written down: it does not assess clinically, does not recommend personal treatment and hands over immediately when the patient asks for a person. Automation provides the speed; people build the trust.

Frequently asked questions

How quickly should a clinic reply to a patient enquiry?

There is no single right number. Minutes during opening hours and first thing the next morning out of hours is a sensible starting point. What matters is that the clinic writes its own standard, measures it for every enquiry and has a back-up plan for when it is missed.

Is an automatic "we've received your message" reply enough?

No. An acknowledgement that does not answer the question is not a reply, and patients rarely see it as one. The first reply should use their name, answer the question briefly and ask about the next step.

What should we do with enquiries that arrive out of hours?

Have a separate plan. Either an AI assistant gives the first reply with approved information, or the patient is given a clear time and contacted first thing in the morning. Track these enquiries on their own line in your weekly report.

Which matters more, speed or the quality of the reply?

They cannot be separated. A fast but empty reply does not answer the question; a full but late reply may arrive after the patient has already chosen another clinic. Aim for a short, genuine answer, sent quickly.
