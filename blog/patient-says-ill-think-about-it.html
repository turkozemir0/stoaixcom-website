"I'll think about it": what to say when a patient stalls

Stalling is the quietest way to lose a patient: they never say no, they just stop replying. The six reasons behind it and how to uncover each one without pushing.

Published: 20 November 20264 min read

Short answer: "I'll think about it" is a curtain, not an answer. Behind it sits one of six reasons: missing information, trust, money, another decision maker, a genuine timing issue or a polite no. One pressure-free question usually reveals which: "Whichever way you decide, what would you need to feel sure about it?"

Why stalling costs so much

A price objection is at least a conversation. A stall is the end of one. The patient thanks you, says they will get back to you and never does. The clinic records "lost on price"; in reality the patient was lost to uncertainty.

Behavioural science has two familiar things to say here. Under uncertainty people pick the default, which is doing nothing. And "later" always looks emptier than it turns out to be. Kahneman and Tversky called this the planning fallacy: we assume the diary will be freer in three months, and it rarely is.

Money"It's not in the budget right now"Talk scope or payment plans, not discounts
Another decision maker"I need to talk to my partner"Bring the partner into the process

How to find the real reason

When you hear the stall, avoid two things: trying to talk them round, and saying "of course, no rush" and closing the topic. Instead:

  1. Acknowledge it. "That makes complete sense, it's a big decision."
  2. Ask one question. "Whichever way you go, what's the one thing you'd need to feel sure?"
  3. Pause. The answer usually names the real reason.

If timing is the issue, gently surface the planning fallacy: "What do you think will be different in three months?" It is not a trick. Sometimes the answer really is "I'll have time off then", and that is a perfectly good answer.

Give the patient permission to say no

It sounds backwards, but one way to reduce pressure is to make saying no easy. "Have you decided to put this on hold for now?" gives the patient a safe exit. When no is allowed, people are more honest: they either share the real concern or give a clear no. A clear no is useful too. It keeps your follow-up list clean and frees your team's time for patients who are genuinely interested.

Create a sense of progress, not waiting

Stalling feeds on a process that looks complicated and vague. Patients should feel they are moving, not waiting:

  • Small yeses. Small steps before the big decision: sending an X-ray, a 10-minute video call with the clinician, looking at available dates.
  • Visible progress. Clear status updates such as "your assessment is with the clinician" or "your plan is ready".
  • Real time benefits. "Send the X-ray today and you'll have your plan tomorrow" motivates more than any discount.

Never use false urgency. "This price ends tomorrow", if it is not true, does lasting damage to trust.

Following up with a patient who stalled

If they gave a real date, plan backwards from it: a reminder two months ahead, practical details one month ahead. A vague stall goes into the follow-up schedule after a quote. Every touch should leave the patient a little better informed or a little more at ease.

Frequently asked questions

Should I offer a discount when a patient says they'll think about it?

No. Stalls usually come from uncertainty, not money. A discount does not solve the real reason and undermines your price.

What if the patient says "I'll get back to you"?

Accept it, but do not leave the follow-up to them: "Of course. Would it help if I sent a quick reminder next week?" A follow-up the patient agreed to is not intrusive.

What if they keep moving the date?

At some point, ask directly and kindly: "Have you decided to put this on hold for now?" A clear answer is better than an open-ended wait.
