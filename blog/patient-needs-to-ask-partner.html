"I need to talk to my partner": winning the decision made at home

Treatment decisions are often made at the kitchen table, not by the person who filled in the form. How to treat this sentence as an invitation rather than an obstacle.

Published: 20 November 20264 min read

Short answer: "I need to talk to my partner" is not an objection. It tells you where the decision will be made. The right move is to bring the partner in, not to work around them: a one-page note written for the partner, a joint video call if they would like one, and a next contact date the patient agrees to.

Who actually decides?

For dental, hair or aesthetic treatment, the person who fills in the form is only part of the decision. When treatment abroad is involved, it is usually a family decision. It helps to separate four roles:

RoleWho it might beWhat they care about
DeciderThe patientOutcome, process, clinician
InfluencerPartner, adult child, close friendSafety, realism
PayerThe patient or the familyTotal cost, no surprises
VetoOften someone you never meet"What if something goes wrong?"

The dangerous role is the invisible veto: someone who has never seen the quote or spoken to the clinician can end it with one sentence. That is why it pays to learn about decision makers before you send the quote, and to note it in the CRM.

Do not ask "who makes the decision?"; it sounds blunt and invites a defensive answer. Ask about the process instead: "How do you usually make decisions like this? Who would you like to go through the plan with?"

The sentence can mean three things

  1. A genuine joint decision. Healthy and common.
  2. A polite hesitation. The patient is unsure about something and uses the partner as a graceful exit.
  3. An unspoken fear. The patient is anxious but names the partner instead of the fear.

One open question separates them: "What do you think your partner will ask first?" A genuine joint decision produces a concrete question. Hesitation or fear usually surfaces the patient's own concern.

The partner's first question is rarely price

Clinics tend to assume the partner will ask "why so expensive?". In practice the first question is usually about safety: "What happens if something goes wrong there?", "Who is the doctor?", "Will you be on your own?" Information written in the partner's language works better than any price justification.

A one-page note for the partner

The patient will be doing the presentation at home, so help them. A good partner note covers:

  • A three-sentence summary of the plan
  • The clinician's name, experience and how they stay in touch
  • The process day by day: how many days, where to stay, who meets them
  • What the clinic does if there is a problem, in writing
  • The total cost and what is included
  • A name and number the partner can contact directly

Grand, vague claims do not reassure a sceptical partner; concrete commitments do. Only write the ones you can keep.

The joint video call

Invite the family in rather than waiting for them: "If it helps, we can do a 15-minute video call with your partner, so they can ask me or the clinician directly." On the call, take the partner's questions before presenting, and shape what you say around them. Offer a few meaningful choices on dates, companions and accommodation; the clinical decision stays with the clinician.

What not to do: cut the partner out

"This price is only valid today" is an attempt to push the person with veto power out of the decision. It almost always ends the same way: the patient goes home, the partner sees the pressure and trust is gone. See our guide to ethical persuasion.

The next contact is a promise

Do not end the conversation without a date: "Shall I message you on Thursday evening, once you've had a chance to talk?" A date the patient agrees to turns follow-up into keeping a promise rather than chasing. For other kinds of stall, see when a patient says "I'll think about it".

Frequently asked questions

When should I message again after "I need to ask my partner"?

On a date the patient agrees to. Ask at the end of the conversation, "Shall I message you on Thursday evening?"; an open-ended wait usually turns into silence.

Will inviting the partner to a call put the patient off?

Not if it is offered as an option. Most patients are relieved not to have to present everything on their own.

What if the partner is against the treatment?

Treat their concern as legitimate and make it concrete. It is usually safety, recovery or being alone. Bring the clinician in for any clinical questions.
