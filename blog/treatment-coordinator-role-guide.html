The treatment coordinator role: duties, daily plan and measures

A treatment coordinator stays with a patient from their first message until the day treatment starts. This guide makes the role concrete: duties, a working day and how to measure it.

Published: 20 November 20268 min read

This article is part of the Patient enquiry management: from first message to booked consultation series.

Short answer: A treatment coordinator owns the patient enquiries that come into a private clinic. They give or take over the first reply, understand the patient's goal and concerns, organise the clinical assessment, present the treatment plan, run follow-up and record the decision. They do not make clinical decisions; they build a trusted bridge between clinician and patient. Their work is measured by response time, progression to assessment, plan-to-decision rate and the quality of the record.

What is a treatment coordinator?

A treatment coordinator is the single point of contact for people asking about treatment at a private clinic, from first contact until treatment begins. In dental practices the title is usually treatment coordinator; in aesthetic and hair restoration clinics it may be patient consultant or patient care coordinator; clinics treating international patients often call it international patient coordinator. The job is essentially the same. It can look like a sales role, but the real work is building trust: taking the patient's question seriously, making the clinician's assessment understandable, explaining fees and process honestly and keeping every promise on time. The difference from reception is focus: not the day's appointments, but patients who are still deciding. The difference from the clinician is that the coordinator does not decide on clinical suitability or treatment choice. In a small practice the role may be a receptionist's second hat for fixed hours of the day; in a growing group it becomes a dedicated person or team. Either way, every enquiry should have one named coordinator.

Job description: five core responsibilities

  1. Owning the enquiry. Keeps the record of every assigned patient open, dates the next task and stays with it until the patient decides.
  2. Understanding the goal and the worry. Finds out why the patient is acting now, what concerns them most and who else is part of the decision, and notes it in the patient's own words.
  3. Bridging clinician and patient. Gathers the photos, X-rays or information the clinician needs, passes them on, gives the patient a clear time for an answer and explains the clinician's view in plain language.
  4. Presenting the plan and following up. Explains the treatment plan starting from the patient's goal, answers questions, follows up on a planned calendar and listens to objections.
  5. Recording and measuring. Logs every conversation in the CRM, records losses with their reasons and prepares the weekly scorecard.

A sample working day

If the coordinator's day is handed over to phone traffic, patients who are deciding always slip to "later". Working in time blocks prevents that. This plan is an example for a clinic with one coordinator working 9am to 6pm:

TimeBlockWhat happens
9:00–9:30Overnight hand-overEnquiries answered by the assistant out of hours are taken over and each gets human contact
9:30–10:00Today's listTasks dated today, patients waiting for a clinician's answer and open plans are reviewed in the CRM
10:00–12:00New enquiries and callsFirst replies, needs conversations, returning missed calls
12:00–12:30Clinician bridgeCases awaiting assessment go to the clinician; finished views are collected
1:30–3:30Plans and follow-upTreatment plan conversations, follow-up messages and calls
3:30–5:00New enquiries and bookingsReplies to the day's enquiries, appointment confirmations
5:00–5:30CloseDoes every open enquiry have a dated task, are loss reasons entered, hand-over note for tomorrow

New enquiries do not wait for the next block, of course; the plan simply ensures that patients who are deciding also get a fixed place in the day.

"Open enquiries without a task" is the simplest and most revealing line: if it is not zero, there is a hole in the process. For formulas in detail, see patient enquiry conversion metrics.

What makes a good treatment coordinator

When hiring, or moving someone from the existing team into the role, look for:

  • Listening. Hearing the real wish behind the patient's first sentence; letting the patient do most of the talking.
  • Written tone. Writing short, warm, error-free messages. Most of the conversation happens in writing.
  • Keeping promises. If they said "I'll come back to you at 2pm tomorrow", they come back at 2pm. That is the most concrete proof of trust.
  • Knowing the limits. Leaving clinical questions to the clinician and never inventing an answer.
  • Record discipline. Writing two lines of notes after every conversation as a habit.
  • Persuasion without pressure. Helping the patient reach an informed decision rather than trying to speed them up.

Sales experience helps but is not essential; aggressive sales habits usually do more harm than good in healthcare.

Hand the draining work to automation

A coordinator's value lies in the human connection with the patient. Replying to a message at 11pm, sending reminders, logging conversations and remembering follow-up dates sit outside that value, yet neglecting them breaks the whole process. At STOAIX that work goes to software: the clinic CRM keeps every patient's owner, stage and next task on one screen, and lead follow-up sends first replies and reminders on time and hands the conversation to the coordinator the moment the patient replies. The coordinator's day is then spent talking to patients, not searching for messages.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a treatment coordinator and a receptionist?

Reception looks after the clinic's daily running: the patient at the desk, the phone and the diary. The treatment coordinator focuses on patients who are still deciding: understanding their needs, organising the assessment, presenting the plan and following up. In a small practice one person can wear both hats; what matters is that time is set aside.

Can a treatment coordinator give clinical information?

They can share general information the clinic has approved, such as the stages of a treatment and what the process involves. Decisions and explanations about personal suitability, treatment choice and risks belong to the clinician. Any question they are unsure about goes to the clinician, with a clear time for an answer.

How many enquiries can one coordinator manage?

There is no single right number; it depends on the number of channels, the type of treatment and how much automation takes on. A practical test: when open enquiries without tasks appear, promised times start to slip and notes get shorter, capacity is full.

Should a treatment coordinator be paid commission?

If you introduce one, link it not only to closed treatment but also to response time, record quality and patient feedback. Commission tied only to sales raises the risk of pushing patients into decisions, which damages trust and sits badly with professional and advertising standards in healthcare.
