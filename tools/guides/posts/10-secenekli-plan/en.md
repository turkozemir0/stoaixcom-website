> **Short answer:** Offer no more than three options and build each one as an answer to a different priority, not as a price tier: the quickest route, the most balanced plan, the longest-lasting solution. All three must be clinician-approved. Always finish the presentation with the clinician's clear recommendation and a single question.

## Why one option is not enough

A single-option quote leaves one question in the patient's mind: "Should I do this or not?" The easiest answer to that is "let me think about it". Three options change the question to "which of these suits me?". The patient is now deciding between your options rather than between you and another clinic.

An option-based plan is also the most honest answer to a budget objection. When a patient says "too expensive", you can move to a different scope instead of cutting the price, as we discuss in [the "too expensive" guide](/blog/patient-says-too-expensive).

## Why three at most?

More options make decisions harder, not easier. The choice overload and decision fatigue that psychologists describe are stronger in healthcare, where the patient is already making an uncertain, anxious decision. Show five options and the patient starts building a spreadsheet, and the decision gets postponed. Two or three clear options are enough.

## Options should answer priorities

Labelling options "economy, standard, premium" reduces them to price tiers. Say which priority each one answers instead:

| Option | Whose priority it answers | Example (dental) |
|---|---|---|
| Quickest | "I want it done as soon as possible" | Completed in one visit, narrower scope |
| Balanced | "I don't want problems later" | The clinician's recommended full plan |
| Comprehensive | "I never want to deal with this again" | Additional treatment, longer warranty |

All three must be options the clinician considers clinically appropriate for this patient. Nothing goes on the list just because it makes selling easier.

## The compromise effect: where honesty ends and manipulation begins

Given three options, people tend to choose the middle one. The effect is real, and used honestly it helps the patient: if the middle option really is the clinician's balanced recommendation, the right decision becomes easier.

It becomes manipulation when you use it to push patients toward something they do not need. The classic form is the decoy: an option so expensive nobody would choose it, added only to make the middle one look reasonable. Patients sense it, and trust goes. The test is simple: would I genuinely recommend this option to this patient?

## What the plan should contain

The plan is the document that sells when you are not in the room; it is read at the kitchen table and next to competing quotes. A good option-based plan includes:

- **The patient's goal in their own words.** "I want to eat comfortably again."
- **What each option gains and what it gives up.** Honestly.
- **What is included and what is not.** "All-inclusive" says nothing; list the items.
- **What still needs confirming.** Do not hide items the clinician says "may be needed"; write them as scenarios so there is no surprise price in the chair.
- **The clinician's clear recommendation.** "For your situation, Dr ... recommends the balanced option because…"
- **One next step.**

Keep small choices such as hotel, room type and transfers out of the plan. They belong to personalisation after the decision; in the plan, they multiply the number of options.

## How to present it

A PDF is fast; a video call builds connection. If you can, read the plan on screen with the patient. Reflect their goal back first, then the options, then the price. Say the price clearly and pause; let the patient speak first. Close with a dated next step and send a written summary within the hour.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to give every patient three options?

No. Sometimes there is only one clinically right answer; then offer one option and explain why. The number of options is set by clinical suitability, not by sales.

### Should options be ordered by price?

Name them by priority and show the price clearly under each. Hiding the price or burying it at the end undermines trust.

### What if the patient chooses the cheapest option?

If it is clinically appropriate, that is a legitimate decision. Make what is not included clear in writing and leave the door open to extending the scope later.
