> **Short answer:** Patient stories are the strongest trust tool you have, but they work best in one-to-one conversations, not in advertising. Collect them with explicit written consent, keep them typical rather than exceptional, anonymise by default and share the right story with the patient who asked. In the UK, testimonials in ads fall under the CAP Code and the ASA; health data falls under UK GDPR.

## Why patient stories work

When deciding, a patient asks three questions: "Will it work? Did it work for someone like me? Will something go wrong?" However well the clinic answers, the experience of a similar person is more convincing. Psychologists call it social proof: under uncertainty, people look at what people like them did.

For patients travelling abroad for treatment, the effect is stronger still. "Someone my age, from my country, had this done and is happy" often matters more than price.

## What the rules say

In the UK, the CAP Code governs marketing communications, including a clinic's website and social media. Testimonials must be genuine, verifiable and must not imply results that are not typical. Claims about medical treatment need evidence, and the ASA regularly rules against before/after images that exaggerate or are edited. Cosmetic procedure marketing must not trivialise risk or target under-18s.

Health information is special category data under UK GDPR, so using a patient's story or images requires explicit consent that specifies how and where it will be used, and that can be withdrawn.

Other markets draw the line in similar places: Germany's Heilmittelwerbegesetz restricts patient testimonials in advertising, and Turkey tightened health advertising rules in November 2025.

## Advertising versus one-to-one conversation

| | Advertising and shop window | One-to-one conversation |
|---|---|---|
| Audience | Everyone | The patient who asked |
| Example | Instagram post, website gallery, ad creative | A similar case sent to a patient who asked on WhatsApp |
| Risk | High: directly subject to advertising rules | Lower, but consent and confidentiality still apply |
| Impact | Diffuse | High: answers the patient's actual question |

When a patient asks, "Can I see a result for someone in my situation?", sharing a consented, suitable case answers a request for information.

## How to collect a good patient story

1. **Consent.** Get written consent that states the channel, the format and the duration of use.
2. **Timing.** Ask when the result is visible and satisfaction is at its highest, usually not the day after treatment.
3. **The patient's own words.** Collect concrete sentences, not "great experience": "Pain was my biggest fear, and the first night was far easier than I expected."
4. **Confidentiality.** Names, faces and identifying details only with explicit consent. Initials and an age group are usually enough.
5. **Typical, not exceptional.** Presenting an outlier result as typical is misleading.

## Match the story to the patient

Social proof comes from similarity. A 25-year-old's smile makeover story will not reassure a 58-year-old implant patient. Organise your story library by treatment, country or language, age group and the patient's main fear (pain, cost, trust, travel). When a patient names their fear, the story of someone who had the same fear is the strongest answer.

## Fake social proof: a hard line

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I share before/after photos on WhatsApp?

Sharing a consented, anonymised case one-to-one when a patient specifically asks is treated differently from public advertising, but consent must cover that use. Take advice for your own situation.

### Are Google reviews subject to advertising rules?

A review a patient writes independently on a third-party platform is not your ad. Republishing reviews on your website or in ads makes them your marketing, so CAP Code rules apply.

### Video or written stories?

A short video or voice story is very powerful one-to-one but carries a heavier consent and privacy burden. Short, well-written, anonymised stories are enough to start.
