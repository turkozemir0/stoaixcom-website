> **Short answer:** "Too expensive" is a comparison word, so first ask what they are comparing it with. Price objections come in four types: a competing quote, a real budget limit, doubt about value and a haggling reflex. Each needs a different move, and none of them starts with a discount.

## An objection is not a rejection

Patients who have decided no do not object; they go quiet. A patient who says "too expensive" is still talking. They want an answer they need in order to decide. So aim to understand the objection, not to defeat it.

A simple sequence works for any objection: **listen, acknowledge, explore, respond.** The most common mistake is jumping straight to the response. "But our implants are Swiss-made" is wasted if you do not know what the patient is comparing you with.

## Find the comparison first

"Expensive" is always expensive compared with something. The question is simple: "What are you comparing it with?" or, more gently, "Did you have a figure in mind?" The answer tells you which of the four types you are facing.

## The four types of price objection

| Type | What the patient says | The right move |
|---|---|---|
| Comparison | "Another clinic quoted less" | Compare the quotes line by line, together |
| Budget | "I can't stretch to that" | Talk about scope or payment plans |
| Doubt | "I'm not sure it's worth it" | Show concretely why the price is what it is |
| Haggling reflex | "Any chance of a discount?" | Give and take: nothing for nothing |

### Comparison

Do not criticise the competitor and do not match the price on the spot. Offer to put the two quotes side by side: which materials or brands, how many reviews, whether aftercare is included, who carries out the treatment, what happens if there is a complication. The difference is usually in scope, not price. Doing this comparison with the patient is far more persuasive than you telling them.

### Budget

If the budget is real, change the scope rather than the price: phased treatment, a different material, a payment plan. One firm line: never suggest a clinically unsuitable option to fit a budget. ### Doubt

A price that is too low creates doubt as well: "How can they do it for that?" You should be able to justify your price in one concrete sentence specific to you. "High-quality care" is not a reason. "Your clinician plans everything from a CT scan and two years of reviews are included" is.

### Haggling reflex

Some patients ask for a discount before they even ask the price, and in some cultures that is simply normal. Treat it as a test. Ask why first. If you give something, get a real step in return: a deposit, a firm date, flexibility on appointment times. A discount given for nothing tells the patient the first price was never real.

## Don't hand the patient your own objection

A hidden problem in many clinic teams: the coordinator privately thinks the price is too high. "I wouldn't pay that" leaks into tone, unnecessary apologies and early discounts before the patient has said a word. Explain to your team why the price is what it is; nobody can defend a price they do not believe in.

## Every answer ends with a small step

Your reply to an objection should end with a small next step: "Would you like to share the other quote so I can put them side by side?" or "Shall I ask the clinician about a phased option?" An answer without a step leaves the conversation hanging.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I offer a discount when a patient says it's too expensive?

No. Ask what they are comparing it with first. Most price objections come from scope that the patient cannot see, not the price itself.

### What should I say if a competitor is much cheaper?

Without criticising them, offer to compare the two quotes line by line: materials, clinician, reviews, what is included and what happens if there is a complication.

### What can I offer a patient whose budget genuinely falls short?

Phased treatment, a different material option or a payment plan. Never a clinically unsuitable option; that decision belongs to the clinician.
