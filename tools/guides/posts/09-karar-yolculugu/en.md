> **Short answer:** Patients contact you at any point in their decision journey: trigger, research, comparison, assessment, decision, pre-treatment and aftercare. Your first job is to work out which stage they are at. Narrow the options for researchers, make comparison easy for shoppers, reduce risk for deciders, and at every stage ask for the next small step rather than the big decision.

## Why patients arrive mid-journey

Patients do not message you the day they notice a problem. They think about it for months, ask friends, read online and may already have quotes. If you do not know where they are when they reach you, you send the wrong stage's message: pressure to a researcher, a brochure to a decider.

## Seven stages

| Stage | Where the patient is | What they need | The right small step |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Trigger | An event made it urgent (a wedding, pain, a photo) | To be understood | "What made you start looking into this now?" |
| 2. Research | Learning the options, confused | Simplicity | A short suitability check |
| 3. Comparison | Has quotes from several clinics | Easy comparison | A clear, itemised quote |
| 4. Assessment | Has sent photos or X-rays | A fast, clear clinical view | A short call with the clinician |
| 5. Decision | Close to yes, fears at their peak | Less risk | A date and a deposit |
| 6. Pre-treatment | Booked, in the weeks of doubt | Visibility and preparation | Countdown messages |
| 7. Aftercare | Recovering | Not to feel alone | Check-ins and a satisfaction question |

## How to tell the stage

Ask directly, naturally:

- "How long have you been thinking about this?"
- "Have you spoken to other clinics?"
- "When are you hoping to have treatment?"

Record the answers in the CRM. The next coordinator or assistant should never make the patient answer the same questions again.

## The most common mistake at each stage

**Too much information during research.** Five treatment options, three package prices and a ten-page brochure delay the decision. The goal at this stage is to narrow.

**A vague quote during comparison.** An "all-inclusive" quote loses next to a competitor's itemised one. Make comparison easy; see [option-based treatment plans](/blog/good-better-best-treatment-plan).

**Pressure at the decision stage.** Patients are most afraid when they are closest to yes. They need reassurance, not pressure: refund policy, a clear schedule, a call with the clinician. This is where [false urgency](/blog/ethical-persuasion-clinic-sales) does most damage.

**Silence after the deposit.** If contact drops off after booking, regret sets in. Pre-treatment is where the decision is tested hardest, not where the sale ends.

## The small-yes principle

Never ask a patient to complete the whole journey in one go. Instead of asking a researcher "shall we book a surgery date?", ask "could you send two photos so we can see whether you're suitable?". Each small step makes the next easier and leaves the patient feeling in control.

## Tracking stages in the CRM

The journey can only be managed when it is visible. Each enquiry record needs the current stage, the next small step and its date. Write the stage definitions down: "quote sent" and "quote discussed" are different stages. With that data you can see where you lose patients; see [clinic patient funnel metrics](/blog/clinic-patient-funnel-metrics).

## Frequently asked questions

### Can patients move backwards between stages?

Yes, often. A patient at the decision stage may see a new quote and return to comparison. The CRM stage should reflect the patient's real situation, not your sales targets.

### Does every patient go through all seven stages?

No. A referred patient may skip research and comparison and come straight to assessment. The stages are a map, not a mandatory sequence.

### Won't asking about their stage put patients off?

Not when it is asked naturally. "Have you spoken to other clinics?" shows you want to help.
