> **Short answer:** Lowball quotes lift conversion in the short term at three costs: eroded margin, cut corners and price-driven patients. Answer a competitor's low price by clarifying the comparison, not by cutting yours: are we comparing like with like? An itemised quote, a clear list of exclusions and an option-based plan let you compete without starting a price war.

## Why a lower quote looks tempting

Most medical travel patients collect several quotes. Sales teams hear "I found somewhere cheaper" from every lost patient, and the instinct is to cut the price. In the short term it works: conversion rises and the team relaxes. The bill arrives months later.

## Three costs of lowballing

| Cost | How it shows up |
|---|---|
| Eroded margin | More patients are needed for the same revenue; team and clinician capacity strain |
| Cut corners | To protect margin, transfers, accommodation, check-ups or materials are trimmed |
| The wrong patients | Price-driven patients negotiate hardest, complain most and refer least |

There is a hidden cost too. A clinic that keeps cutting prices becomes known as the cheap clinic, and that perception is very hard to change later.

## When a patient says "I found it cheaper"

First, check whether the comparison is like for like. The same treatment name can mean very different things:

- How many implants, which brand, which restoration?
- How many grafts, who performs the procedure, which technique?
- How many nights, are transfers and check-ups included?
- What happens if there is a complication, and who pays?

Ask helpfully, not suspiciously: "To help you compare properly, shall we go through what's included in the other quote together?"

Patients often do not know what the other quote leaves out. The conversation makes the difference visible without criticising anyone.

## The itemised quote is your strongest defence

An "all-inclusive" quote loses to a lower number. An itemised quote shows what the patient pays for. A good quote includes every treatment item and material, included services, exclusions written out, "may be needed" items as scenarios, and aftercare and warranty terms.

## Options instead of discounts

If the budget really is limited, change the scope rather than the price. A narrower version of the same treatment is a far more honest answer than a discount. See [when a patient says it's too expensive](/blog/patient-says-too-expensive) and [option-based treatment plans](/blog/good-better-best-treatment-plan).

## Losing some patients is right

You do not have to win every patient. Someone who decides only on the lowest price will leave the moment they see a cheaper quote. Losing them costs less than lowering your price for everyone. What matters is recording why you lost each patient so you can tell a real price problem from a communication problem.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it ever right to price-match?

Occasionally, if you know the scope and quality really are the same and your margin allows it. As a system, though, it ties your prices to your competitors' decisions.

### What if the patient won't share the other quote?

Don't push. Send your own quote as a clear checklist of what is included; the patient can compare it themselves.

### Should we publish prices on our website?

A starting price range helps patients filter early. The exact price should come after assessment, itemised.
