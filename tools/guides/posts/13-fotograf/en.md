> **Short answer:** Ask for photos when the answer to the patient's own question depends on them: "Am I a candidate?", "How many grafts would I need?". A good request has five parts: context, reason, who will see the photos, what happens next and how to make it easy. Give a clinician-approved numbered list, check the set before passing it on and keep health data in a secure clinic system, not on personal phones.

## Why photos are a trust threshold

Before paying anything, the patient shows you part of their body: hair loss, teeth, face, body. The more intimate the image, the more relationship you need first. "Send us some photos" in the first message is too early for many patients.

## The right moment

The most natural moment is when the patient's own question depends on the photo:

- "Am I suitable for implants?"
- "How many grafts would I need?"
- "Which technique would work for my nose?"

At that point the request is not a hurdle but the way to answer them: "To answer that properly, our clinician needs to see a few photos."

## Five parts of a good request

| Part | Example |
|---|---|
| Context | "So we can assess whether you're a good candidate…" |
| Reason | "…our surgeon needs to see your donor area and the area of thinning." |
| Who sees them | "Only Dr Shah and your patient coordinator will see your photos." |
| What happens next | "I'll come back to you with the surgeon's view by midday tomorrow." |
| Ease and choice | "A phone camera is fine. If you'd prefer, we can do a short video call first." |

## A numbered, specific list

"Send a few photos" brings back unusable ones. Give a short, numbered list approved by the clinician:

- **Hair:** front, top, both sides, back (donor area); good light, dry hair.
- **Dental:** a recent panoramic X-ray if available, smiling from the front, teeth closed from the front.
- **Face and nose:** front, both profiles, both three-quarter angles; no make-up, neutral expression.
- **Body:** no face, through the secure channel the patient prefers.

For some specialties (bariatric, eye surgery), assessment relies more on measurements and health information than photos; check with the clinician first.

## Check the set

The coordinator does not assess the photos but checks the set is complete. If something is missing, ask only for that, with thanks.

## Come back when you said you would

Thank the patient as soon as photos arrive and give a clear return time with a little buffer. Keeping that promise is trust itself. If there will be a delay, say so before the deadline. Pass on the clinician's view without changing it, make it understandable, and be clear that a pre-assessment is not a final plan.

## Patients who don't send photos

A patient who doesn't send photos is usually not uninterested but unsure. Offer an alternative: a short video call with the clinician, general information first or a clear explanation of how photos are stored. Do not close the record. ## Privacy and data security

Health data is special category data under UK GDPR. The biggest risks are usually in someone's pocket: personal WhatsApp accounts, phone galleries backed up to the cloud, case photos shared in team chats and images uploaded to unapproved tools. You need to be able to tell the patient who will see the photos, where they are stored and how they are deleted, with a real process behind the answer. A deletion request is a right; action it the same day.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it wrong to ask for photos in the first message?

Unless the patient asks, it is usually too early. Understand their question and expectations first, then ask for photos as the way to answer.

### Is WhatsApp safe for receiving photos?

Messages are end-to-end encrypted, but the real risk is photos remaining on personal phones, galleries and backups. Use a business account and a process that moves photos straight into the patient record.

### Can we give a price after a photo assessment?

A clinician-approved range, yes, with a clear statement that the final price is confirmed after examination.
