> **Short answer:** Responding to a patient objection takes four steps: listen without interrupting, acknowledge that the concern is reasonable, ask an open question to find the real cause, and only then give an honest answer to that cause. "It's too expensive", "I'll think about it" and "I'm getting other quotes" can each hide different reasons; an answer given before the reason is found usually answers the wrong question. Clinical concerns are always passed to the clinician.

## What is a patient objection?

A patient objection is a concern, question or hesitation that an interested patient raises before deciding on treatment. In sales language "objection" sounds like an obstacle; in healthcare it is often the sign of a healthy decision. A patient who asks nothing before a procedure in their mouth, on their face or affecting the family budget has probably not really thought about it yet. Objections usually fall into five groups: cost, trust, timing, fear and decision authority. An answer given without knowing the group makes the patient feel unheard. This part gives your team a shared library and a conversation framework for meeting objections the same way. The aim is never to persuade someone into treatment they do not want; it is to resolve the open question of a patient who does want treatment but is stuck on something.

## Four steps: listen, acknowledge, ask, respond

This framework appears under different names in sales training, and it fits healthcare conversations well:

| Step | What happens | Example line |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Listen | No interrupting; the objection is heard in full | (silence, taking notes) |
| 2. Acknowledge | Accept the concern is reasonable; understanding, not agreeing | "It makes complete sense to think carefully about a decision like this." |
| 3. Ask | An open question to find the real cause | "Which part is giving you the most to think about?" |
| 4. Respond | A short, honest answer to that specific cause | "If it's the payment side, let me explain the options." |

The most common mistake is skipping steps two and three and jumping straight to an answer. Offering a discount or a quality speech to someone who says "it's expensive" may leave their real question, "do I actually need this?", unanswered.

## Six causes behind "I'll think about it"

"I'll think about it" is the most frequent and least informative reply. It usually hides one of six causes:

| Cause | Clue | Question | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Budget | Silence after the price | "Is it the payment side, or something else?" | Explain payment options |
| 2. Needs to talk to partner or family | "I'll ask my husband" | "What do you think they'll most want to know?" | Send a family summary, offer a joint call |
| 3. Lack of trust | Questions about clinician, clinic or results | "Is there anything about us you'd like to be clearer on?" | Short call with the clinician, a visit |
| 4. Fear | Questions on pain, recovery, risk | "Which part of the procedure worries you?" | Clinician explains the process |
| 5. Timing | Work, holidays, an event | "When would be a good time for you?" | Open a task for that date |
| 6. Comparison | A quote from another clinic | "What are you comparing?" | A like-for-like scope checklist |

Asking for the reason directly is not rude. Asked gently, it often relaxes patients, because they no longer have to raise the concern themselves.

## The objection library

This table can become your team's shared language. The responses are directions, not scripts; each patient gets an answer in the coordinator's own words.

| Objection | Likely real cause | Direction of the response | Response to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| "It's too expensive." | Budget, unclear value or comparison | Clarify scope and inclusions; payment options | An unprompted discount |
| "It's cheaper elsewhere." | Does not know the scope difference | "Let's compare what's included" checklist | Criticising the other clinic |
| "I need to talk to my partner." | Joint decision | Family summary, offer of a joint call | "It's your decision" pressure |
| "I don't have time right now." | Real timing or priority | Agree a suitable date together | A fake offer deadline |
| "Will it hurt?" | Fear | Clinician explains the process and comfort options | "It won't hurt at all" |
| "Is the result guaranteed?" | Worry about uncertainty | Clinician sets realistic expectations | A promised outcome |
| "I'll check the reviews first." | Trust | Suggest a visit or meeting the clinician | Sending curated or fake reviews |
| "I'll get back to you." | Unclear | "When would you like me to message?" to get a date | Waiting for them to come back |

Lines such as "it won't hurt at all" or "the result is guaranteed" are not honest and risk breaching the [CAP Code](https://www.asa.org.uk/codes-and-rulings/advertising-codes/non-broadcast-code.html) rules on misleading claims and on medical and cosmetic marketing. Questions about clinical outcomes, risks and pain go to the clinician.

## A note on price objections

A price objection is usually not about the number but about value: the patient cannot fully see what they get for it. The first step is to make scope clear: which procedures, which reviews, which materials and what aftercare is included. If there are payment options, explain them plainly. More in [answering patient price questions](/blog/answering-patient-price-questions). An unprompted discount weakens the credibility of the first figure and teaches future patients to expect the same negotiation.

## Where objections go unspoken: silence

The hardest objection is the one never voiced. A patient who reads a message and does not reply has an objection they have not shared. Follow-up messages then work best when they gently open a likely objection rather than asking "have you decided?": "Some patients like to see the payment plan at this point; would that be helpful for you too?" Ways of working with silent patients are in [unresponsive patient enquiries](/blog/unresponsive-patient-enquiries).

## Logging objections as a team

If objections are discussed once and forgotten, nobody learns. Logging each objection in the CRM with a tag does two things: the coordinator knows where to pick up next time, and the team sees at month end which objection comes up most. If "trust" appears often, the problem may not be the phone call at all but how clinicians and the clinic are presented on the website. These logs are the most useful agenda item in the weekly [conversion metrics](/blog/patient-enquiry-conversion-metrics) review.

## A short role-play

The dialogue below is fictional and shows how the four steps flow.

Patient: "I've got the price, but I'll need to think about it."
Coordinator: "Of course, it's natural to think a decision like this through. Which part is giving you the most to think about, the payment side or the treatment itself?"
Patient: "Honestly, the recovery. I don't know whether I'd need time off work."
Coordinator: "That's a really good question. Our dentist is the right person to explain it; shall I book a ten-minute call with her tomorrow?"

The coordinator did not defend the price, did not promise an outcome and passed the clinical question to the clinician. The objection looked like price; the real cause was timing.

## What an AI assistant does and does not do with objections

STOAIX's [AI receptionist](/en/ai-receptionist) meets objections arriving out of hours or at busy times with the same four steps: it listens, acknowledges the concern, asks for the cause and answers with information the clinic has approved. Questions about scope, payment options and appointment availability can be answered straight away this way. Clinical questions about pain, risk or outcomes are not answered by the assistant; the patient is handed to the clinician or coordinator and the objection is logged in the CRM with a tag.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should we say first when a patient says "it's too expensive"?

The first line should acknowledge, not defend: "It's a significant cost, so it makes sense to think about it." Then ask what feels expensive; sometimes it is the total, sometimes how it is paid, sometimes a comparison with another quote. Answer the cause you find.

### Is it right to push a patient who says "I'll think about it"?

Clarifying is right; pushing is not. Gently asking what they will be thinking about and agreeing a date for the next contact respects the patient. If they have clearly made a decision, even a no, respect it.

### Should objection responses be memorised?

Know the direction of each response, not the words. Memorised lines sound scripted to patients. The most useful exercise for a team is a short weekly role-play using objections from real conversations.

### Can a coordinator answer clinical objections?

Coordinators answer questions about process, appointments and payment. Clinical questions about pain, risk, suitability and expected results should always go to the clinician.
