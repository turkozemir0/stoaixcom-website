> **Short answer:** A good first message has five parts: the patient's name, who you are, one detail from their enquiry, a small piece of value and a single question they can answer in five seconds. Price lists, PDFs, photo requests and vague claims like "leading clinic" do not belong in it.

## Why does the first message matter so much?

What we hear first about someone colours how we read everything after it. Kahneman calls this the halo effect, and patients are not immune. If your first message is warm, specific and personal, the price that comes later reads as a reasonable offer. If it is a copy-pasted brochure, the same price reads as "a clinic that sees me as a number".

So the first message is not a greeting. It is a lens.

## The five parts of a good first message

| Part | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Name | Lifts the patient out of the crowd | "Hi Sarah," |
| Who you are | Turns the clinic into a person | "I'm Dan, one of the patient coordinators here." |
| Context | Shows you read the enquiry | "You asked about implants and bone loss." |
| A small piece of value | Gives them something straight away | "A recent X-ray usually answers that question clearly." |
| One question | Hands the conversation back | "Do you have an X-ray from the last year or so?" |

The one-question rule is the one clinics break most often. A message with three questions says "sit down and write me a proper reply", so it gets saved for later, and later often never comes.

## How the first message changes by channel

**Web form.** The patient has probably forgotten they filled it in, and may have filled in several. Your first line should remind them: which page, which treatment.

**WhatsApp.** They came with a question. Answer it first, then ask yours. Skipping their question to get to your own agenda is the fastest way to lose trust.

**Instagram DM.** The shortest channel. Two or three lines. Do not try to explain everything here; offer to continue on WhatsApp or a short call if they would like.

## What never goes in a first message

- **A price list.** Sending prices to someone who did not ask reduces the conversation to price. If they did ask, give an honest range instead of dodging; see our [guide to the price question](/blog/patient-asks-price-first-message).
- **A PDF or brochure.** Nobody reads it, and it feels like a mass mailing.
- **A request for photos.** Asking for personal photos before any trust exists is too early.
- **Vague praise.** "Award-winning", "best in class" and similar lines say nothing. In the UK, superiority claims in healthcare advertising also need to stand up under the CAP Code.

## Three channels, three first messages

**Form (dental implants):** "Hi James, I'm Dan, a patient coordinator at the clinic. You got in touch through our implants page last night. If you can tell me whether it's one tooth or several, I can explain the options that would actually apply to you."

**WhatsApp (hair transplant, "how many grafts would I need?"):** "Hi Tom, good question. It depends on the area and the density of your donor zone, so a range is more honest than a single number. To give you a clearer idea, is the thinning mostly at the front or at the crown?"

**Instagram (aesthetics, "can I get some info?"):** "Hi, of course. Let me know which treatment you're interested in and I'll give you a quick overview. Happy to move to WhatsApp if that's easier."

## What happens after the first message?

If the patient replies, the second message has one job: take their answer seriously and move one step forward. If they do not, the first 48 hours need a rhythm, which we cover in our [speed-to-lead guide](/blog/speed-to-lead-clinic-first-5-minutes). Every follow-up should bring something new.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a first message be?

Three or four short sentences for WhatsApp and forms, two or three lines for Instagram. If it fits on a phone screen without scrolling, it is about right.

### Should I use the clinic's name or my own?

Both. Patients want to talk to a person, not an institution. "I'm Dan, a patient coordinator at the clinic" gives them both in one line.

### Is it wrong to use templates?

The structure can be a template; the message cannot. Keep the five-part skeleton fixed and rewrite the context and question for every enquiry.

### What if the patient asks about price in their very first message?

Do not skip it. Give an honest range, explain what it depends on and ask one easy question. Dodging sends the patient straight back to comparison shopping.
