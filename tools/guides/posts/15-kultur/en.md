> **Short answer:** Look at five axes: is the patient direct or indirect, formal or informal, do they want detail or a summary, do they decide alone or with family, and what is their trust based on? Write the first message to the country profile, then mirror the patient's own style from the second message on. A culture profile is a hypothesis; if the patient behaves differently, follow the patient. Communicating in the patient's own language is, in most markets, the first sign of trust.

## Why culture matters

A coordinator may message patients from the UK, Germany, the Gulf and the Netherlands on the same day. One template for everyone builds trust with some and distance with others. Culture shapes not what the patient says but how they say it and what they need.

## Five axes

| Axis | One end | Other end | Practical question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Communication | Direct ("What's the price?") | Indirect (relationship first) | Do they get straight to the point? |
| Address | Formal (surname, title) | Informal (first name) | How do they introduce themselves? |
| Information | Detail (every item, every step) | Summary (outcome and recommendation) | How do they react to long messages? |
| Decision | Individual | Family | Who do they mention? |
| Trust | Documents, accreditation, process | Relationships, referrals | Which proof do they ask for? |

## Market examples (as hypotheses)

These are generalisations and only a starting point:

- **UK:** polite, often indirect, wary of hype, first names. Over-enthusiastic language ("amazing results!") can reduce trust; a calm, realistic tone works.
- **Germany:** detail and process, formal address (Herr/Frau and surname), clear itemised information. Vague answers hurt trust; written documents are valued.
- **Netherlands:** very direct. Clear question, clear answer; roundabout explanations frustrate.
- **Gulf countries:** relationship first, decisions often involve family, respectful and warm language. A relative may speak on the patient's behalf. Rushing backfires.
- **France:** strong language sensitivity; communicating in French is a serious trust signal.

## First message to the profile, second to the patient

Start with the profile: "Sehr geehrte Frau Schmidt" for a German patient, "Hi Sarah" for a British one. Then mirror the reply. If the German patient writes "Hallo, ich bin Anna", continue informally; if the British patient asks long, detailed questions, give detail.

Passport, language and city do not always point to the same culture. Don't assume; observe and, when in doubt, ask: "Which language would you prefer me to write in?"

## Family decisions and companions

Where decisions are family decisions, bringing the decision-maker in speeds things up. But whoever speaks for the family, clinical information, consent and the final decision belong to the patient. The companion question is often not about hotels but "will I be alone?". See [the patient who needs to ask their partner](/blog/patient-needs-to-ask-partner).

## Language and time zones

Messages in the patient's own language build trust; messages that read like machine translation do the opposite. Mind time zones too: replying instantly to a patient's midnight message builds trust, writing to them at their midnight does not.

## Frequently asked questions

### Isn't relying on culture profiles a cliché?

It is if you treat them as rules. A profile is only a hypothesis for the first message; the patient's actual behaviour always wins.

### Do we need native speakers for every language?

Not a full-time hire per language, but patients should receive natural, error-free messages in their own language. AI assistants can extend your team's capacity considerably here.

### What if a relative speaks on the patient's behalf?

Be respectful and warm with them, but make sure you speak directly with the patient about clinical information and consent.
