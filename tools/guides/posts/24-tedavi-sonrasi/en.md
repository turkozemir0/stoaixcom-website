> **Short answer:** The goal of aftercare communication is to stop the patient feeling abandoned. Write to them before they write to you: daily for the first 72 hours, weekly for the first month, then at check-up dates. Use a written, clinician-approved list that separates normal symptoms from those that need the clinician. Measure satisfaction and solve problems fast; only then ask for a review or referral.

## The biggest risk: feeling abandoned

Before treatment, every message got a reply within minutes. If that speed disappears afterwards, the patient's first thought is "they took the money and forgot me". Even mild swelling grows with that feeling. For international patients it's stronger still: getting back to the clinic isn't easy, and the dentist at home may criticise the work.

The difference between a satisfied patient and one who recommends you is often not the treatment itself but how they felt afterwards.

## The contact plan

| When | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Evening of treatment | Reassurance | "How are you feeling? Tonight's reminders are attached." |
| Days 1–3 | Monitoring | A short daily question, a photo if needed |
| Week 1 | Recovery check | Clinician-approved care information |
| Month 1 | Satisfaction | A short satisfaction question |
| Months 3 and 6 | Check-up reminders | Remote check-up or local referral |
| Year 1 | Relationship | Annual check-up, guarantee information |

For patients flying home, the travel day is its own touchpoint: "Home safely? Three things to watch after the flight are attached."

## A clinician-approved escalation list

Coordinators and assistants don't make clinical judgements. They use a written list approved by your clinicians:

- **Normal symptoms:** such as mild swelling or sensitivity within the expected period. Inform the patient and keep following up.
- **Refer to the clinician:** such as increasing pain, fever or unexpected bleeding. Pass to the clinician the same day.
- **Emergencies:** direct the patient to emergency services and inform the clinic immediately.

Your clinicians should prepare the list per treatment type.

## When something goes wrong

A fast reply matters as much as the problem. Don't play it down, say clearly when the clinician will respond and keep that promise. A well-handled problem can build more trust than no problem at all.

## How advocates are made

When a patient says they're happy, that's the moment to ask for a review or referral; see [when to ask patients for reviews](/blog/when-to-ask-patients-for-reviews). For future treatments, keep the relationship going with [patient reactivation](/en/patient-reactivation).

## Frequently asked questions

### If a patient doesn't write after treatment, does that mean all is well?

Not always. Some patients never voice dissatisfaction; they just don't recommend you. That's why you should write first.

### Can AI answer symptom questions?

Only by sharing clinician-approved general information and escalating. Diagnosis and treatment decisions always belong to the clinician.

### How long should aftercare last?

It depends on the treatment, but a plan of at least one year is a good start for both patient safety and the relationship.
