> **Short answer:** Working with unresponsive patient enquiries is not about repeating the same message. It is about a "silence ladder" that changes tone and frequency over time: frequent, personal contact in the first three days; fewer, more informative messages up to week two; a monthly nurture rhythm after that; and finally a respectful closing message. The closing message tells the patient the door stays open, and it is often the message that gets the most replies. If the patient says no or withdraws consent, everything stops.

## Who is an unresponsive patient enquiry?

An unresponsive patient enquiry is someone who showed interest once, by filling in a form, sending a message or calling, but has not replied to the clinic's later messages or calls. Every clinic builds up a pile of them, and they are usually marked "not interested" and forgotten. Yet silence is rarely about lack of interest. The message was read at a busy moment and lost; the patient is waiting to speak with their partner; they are planning the budget for a few months' time; or they have a question but feel awkward asking it. Managing silence has two ends. At one end, not following up leaves an interested patient to a competitor. At the other, sending the same message again and again irritates the patient and harms the clinic's reputation. The right path is a sequence that changes tone and frequency over time, gives the patient something at each step and ends with a respectful close.

## Four phases: hot, warm, nurture, reactivation

Different time windows call for different behaviour. The framework below is a starting point; adjust the intervals to your own treatments and data.

| Phase | Timing | Frequency | Tone | Goal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hot | 0–72 hours | Several times a day across channels | Personal, fast | Start the first conversation |
| Warm | Days 4–14 | Every two to three days | Informative, helpful | Find the question or objection |
| Nurture | Days 15–90 | Weekly or fortnightly | Valuable, no pressure | Stay in mind |
| Reactivation | After day 90 | Monthly or seasonal | With a genuine new reason | Reopen the door |

In the hot phase the goal is speed; how to answer first is covered in [speed to lead for clinics](/blog/speed-to-lead-clinics). In the warm phase the goal is to find out why the patient went quiet. In nurture, the goal is not to sell but to make sure the clinic comes to mind when the patient is ready.

## The silence ladder

The silence ladder is a sequence that moves the tone of messages up one step each time there is no reply. Each step tries something different from the last.

| Step | Approach | Example message |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | An easy question | "Hi Sarah, would mornings or afternoons suit you better?" |
| 2 | Change of channel | A short voice note or a call instead of a text |
| 3 | Open a likely objection | "Some patients like to see the payment options at this point; shall I send them over?" |
| 4 | Useful content | A short guide to the treatment process or common questions |
| 5 | Offer choices | "What feels right for you now: more information, an initial chat, or me getting back to you later?" |
| 6 | Closing message | The example below |

The name in the message is fictional. Going back to the goal the patient first wrote about makes each step personal.

## The closing message

The closing message is the last message, telling the patient kindly that the clinic is pausing follow-up and leaving the door open. For example:

"Hi Sarah, I've messaged a few times and now may not be the right time, which is completely understandable. So I don't crowd your inbox, I'll pause my messages here. If you'd like to pick this up in future, just reply to this number; your plan and notes are saved with us. Wishing you well."

Its strength is that it carries no pressure. The patient does not feel they are about to lose something, but knows the door is open. Some patients explain their silence at exactly this point. Avoid phrases such as "last chance" or "this price ends soon"; under the [CAP Code](https://www.asa.org.uk/codes-and-rulings/advertising-codes/non-broadcast-code.html), false urgency in marketing is treated as misleading.

## When to stop following up

Follow-up stops immediately when:

- The patient clearly says they are not interested.
- The patient withdraws consent or unsubscribes. In the UK, electronic marketing messages fall under [PECR](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guide-to-pecr/), and an opt-out must be honoured.
- The patient says they have been treated elsewhere.

In these cases mark the record "closed" in the CRM, note the reason and remove it from automated sequences. Patients who do not reply after the closing message move to a reactivation list; months later, when there is a genuine new reason, such as a new clinician joining or a new service starting, a single kind message can check in again. Winning back past patients and enquiries is the job of [patient reactivation](/en/patient-reactivation).

## Common mistakes with silence

- **Copying the same message.** The third "Hi, just checking in" only irritates.
- **Blaming tone.** "We've tried to reach you several times" puts the patient on the defensive.
- **Heavy out-of-hours messaging.** A sales message at midnight damages trust; choose sensible hours unless it is a genuine health matter.
- **Not logging the silence.** If nobody records which step and which channel met silence, the next person starts again from scratch.
- **Never closing.** Follow-up that drifts on indefinitely helps neither the patient nor the team.

## Measuring the ladder

To see which step of the ladder works, track the reply rate after each step separately. If one step gets almost no replies compared with your own history, change its message; if one clearly works better than the others, try moving it earlier. The wider measurement framework is in [patient enquiry conversion metrics](/blog/patient-enquiry-conversion-metrics).

## A fictional example

The flow below is fictional. A patient fills in a form for a filling on Wednesday evening. The clinic writes within ten minutes and calls the next morning; no answer. On day three a short voice note goes out; silence again. On day seven a message offers payment options, and on day ten a short guide to the treatment. On day fourteen the closing message goes out. Two days later the patient replies: their mother was in hospital that week, and now they would like to book. It would have been easy to close this patient as "not interested"; a respectful ladder brought them back.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many messages should we send to an unresponsive patient?

There is no fixed number. What matters is that each message tries something different and that the sequence ends with a respectful closing message. If the patient says no or withdraws consent, stop straight away.

### Is it better to call or message a silent patient?

Try both; some patients prefer messages, others calls. The second step of the silence ladder is exactly that: change channel. The channel a patient first used is usually the one they prefer.

### Does the closing message really work?

Because it carries no pressure, the closing message lands differently and prompts some patients to explain their silence. You can see whether it works in your own clinic by tagging replies that arrive after the closing message.

### Is it right to message a patient who went quiet months ago?

Yes, if consent is still valid and there is a genuine new reason to get in touch. A message with no new reason, such as "are you still interested?", rarely gets a response.
