> **Short answer:** Answer the price question in three parts: say what the fee depends on, share an honest range or starting point where you can, and ask one easy question that gets you closer to an accurate figure. A bare figure ends the conversation; dodging with "please book a consultation" sends the patient elsewhere. Any figure you give, in a message or on your website, must be realistic and not misleading.

## What is the price question really asking?

"How much is it?" is rarely just a request for a number. With that question the patient is trying to find out three things: is this treatment for someone like me, can I afford it, and will this clinic be straight with me? In private healthcare, fees are not like a price tag on a standard product; the scope of the same treatment varies from person to person, and it is often impossible to give an exact figure without a clinical assessment. Patients know this to some extent. That is why the answer to a price question is less a transfer of information than a test of trust. An evasive reply makes the patient think the clinic is hiding something. A bare figure sends them back to a comparison spreadsheet and the conversation ends: they take the number, line it up against other clinics' numbers and try to pick the lowest. A good price answer takes the question seriously and also moves the conversation towards where the fee is actually set: the assessment.

## Why a bare figure and a dodge both fail

| Type of answer | Example | What the patient thinks | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bare figure | "Implants are £X." | "I'll ask a few others." | Comparison; conversation ends |
| Dodge | "Book a consultation and we'll discuss it." | "Why won't they tell me?" | Loss of trust; patient goes quiet |
| Price list | A PDF of every treatment | "I don't know which applies to me." | Confusion |
| Three-part answer | "Depends on + range + one question" | "They answered me, let's carry on." | Conversation continues |

## The three-part price answer

Answer the price question with the same skeleton every time:

1. **What it depends on.** Name two or three concrete factors that set the fee. For example: "The fee depends on how many implants you need, the bone levels and the type of restoration on top."
2. **An honest frame.** If your clinic's policy allows, give a realistic range or a starting point for a single item; if it does not, say so and explain why. The range should reflect fees you actually charge; do not use an artificially low "from" price as a hook.
3. **One easy question.** Ask about the next small step towards an accurate figure. For example: "Do you have a recent X-ray from your dentist?"

This answers the question, gives any figure with its context and moves the conversation towards assessment.

## Example answers by treatment

These examples are fictional and show the skeleton. Figures are left in brackets; fill them with your clinic's real information.

**Dental implants:**

> The fee depends on how many implants you need, your bone levels and the type of crown or bridge on top. For a single implant and crown, our plans usually come to [range], though it can change if bone grafting is needed. So I can give you an accurate figure: do you have a recent X-ray from your dentist?

**Hair restoration:**

> The fee is based on the number of grafts and the condition of your donor area, which our surgeon assesses from photos. If you can send a few photos of the front, crown and back, we can send you a personal plan with clear figures. Would it be easy for you to send them here?

**Aesthetic treatment:**

> The fee depends on the area treated and the amount of product used, which becomes clear at a face-to-face assessment with the practitioner. Let me explain how the process works first, and then we can find a time for an assessment. Have you had this treatment before?

## What to avoid when answering price questions

- **Withholding a figure the patient keeps asking for.** If they insist and your policy allows a range, give it. Hiding it damages trust.
- **Answering with discounts or offers.** Time-limited deals shift attention from the treatment to the price and are exactly the kind of pressure the [CAP Code](https://www.asa.org.uk/codes-and-rulings/advertising-codes/non-broadcast-code.html) and professional regulators scrutinise in healthcare marketing.
- **Running down other clinics.** "Don't go somewhere cheap, you'll regret it" frightens the patient and sounds untrustworthy. Explain your difference through your own process.
- **Fixing a price without a written plan.** An exact figure given before assessment that changes at consultation causes a loss of trust.
- **Leaving the patient hanging.** Ending the conversation after giving fee information, without a question, is one of the most common reasons patients go quiet.

## Price information and the rules in the UK

Price is one of the areas the ASA looks at most in private healthcare marketing. Some practical points:

- Prices on your website, social media and ads count as marketing and should not mislead. A "from" price should be one that a meaningful share of patients actually pay, and what is and is not included should be clear.
- Avoid time-limited offers and promotions that pressure people into a medical decision, especially for cosmetic procedures.
- Once a patient has been assessed, the treatment plan should set out fees and options in writing so they can make an informed decision and compare like with like.
- Messages you send in reply to a patient's own question are a conversation, not a marketing campaign; but sending promotional messages later needs consent under PECR.

These notes are not legal advice; check your price communication with your own adviser.

## When the patient says "that's expensive"

After hearing the fee, a patient may say it is expensive. That is not the end but a new stage of the conversation. The first job is to understand what they are comparing it with: another clinic's quote, their budget, or the figure they had in mind? Each needs a different answer. Detailed responses are in [handling patient objections](/blog/patient-objections-handling).

## Standardise the price answer

Because price is the most common question, it is also where inconsistency shows most: one team member gives a range and another does not, one mentions a discount and another invites the patient in. To prevent that:

1. Write down the clinic's price policy for each main treatment: will a range be given, and based on what?
2. Write a three-part answer template for each treatment together with the team.
3. Agree with the clinicians when the range may change and add that to the template.
4. Each month, read a few conversations that started with price and check whether the template is working.

The STOAIX [AI receptionist](/en/ai-receptionist) answers price questions with the same structure every time, within the policy the clinic has approved, guides the patient towards assessment and hands the conversation to the coordinator. The price policy itself and any negotiation always stay with the team.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should we give prices over the phone?

Explain what the fee depends on and share a realistic frame within the clinic's written policy. The exact figure should come after assessment, in a written treatment plan. A figure given on the phone that changes at consultation damages trust.

### Should we raise price if the patient doesn't ask?

Yes, as the conversation moves on, at the assessment and plan stage. There is no need to raise it unprompted in the first reply, but learning early about any budget concern helps keep the plan realistic.

### What do we say to a patient who asks for a discount?

First understand the concern behind the request: budget, another quote or doubts about value? If the clinic offers payment options, explain them. Using discounts as a marketing lever can conflict with advertising standards, so keep the price policy written and consistent.

### What if another clinic quoted less?

Without criticising the other clinic, help the patient put the two quotes side by side: which items are included, which materials, what aftercare and guarantees apply? Patients often realise at this point that they were not comparing the same thing. The decision always stays with them.
